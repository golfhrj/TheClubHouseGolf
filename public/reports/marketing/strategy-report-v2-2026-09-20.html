Product Marketing Context

This is a two-sided marketplace. Every audience-facing section below is split into Side A — Golfers (demand) and Side B — Partners (supply). They are different buyers with different language, different proof, and different conversion actions. Do not blend them into one "customer." Partner-side copy never goes on a golfer-facing page, and golfer-side copy never carries a partner pitch.

Product Overview

One-liner: Everything Golf. One Clubhouse. — the all-in-one destination for discovering, comparing and buying everything golf.

What it does: Clubhouse Golf brings equipment, apparel, technology, instruction, fitting, travel, courses, services and experiences into one connected ecosystem. Golfers discover their options, understand them, compare them, and buy what is right for their game — on Clubhouse, through Clubhouse's own checkout, for every item from an integrated partner. On the other side, brands, courses, instructors and creators reach golfers throughout that decision journey and have orders delivered to them to fulfil.

Product category: Golf discovery and commerce platform. Externally: "the destination for everything golf." The long-term ambition is to own a new category rather than sit on an existing shelf — the mental shortcut Google : search :: Amazon : buy :: Clubhouse : anything golf.

Product type: Two-sided marketplace with owned checkout, content-led. Clubhouse is merchant of record but holds no inventory — partners fulfil. Not a retailer, not a content site, not an affiliate publisher.

What owning checkout means operationally. No inventory and no warehouse — but from day one CHG runs payments, refunds, chargebacks, sales tax as merchant of record, order routing to partners, dispatch and tracking visibility, customer service for every completed order, and partner payouts. Phase 1 is not "operationally light." It is inventory-light.

Why the affiliate networks can't carry Phase 1. The Marketplace Report verified against CJ's production API that neither CJ nor Awin has a checkout, cart or order mutation — every link redirects to the advertiser's own site. So every Clubhouse-completed order depends on a direct partner integration signed outside the networks. The networks remain useful for product data and for the referral line.

Referral benchmarks in hand (parallel line only): Golf Galaxy 10% (website purchase), TGW 6% (advanced sale), Pine Meadow Golf 10% (CJ), Puma/Cobra Golf 4% (CJ), Performance Golf 25% (FlexOffers), Steadfast Golf 10–15%, 2nd Swing 5–10%, Live Forever Golf 0–10%, Amazon Associates sports 3%. Payment timing runs ~60–80 days.

Settlement model — diverges from the Marketplace Report. That document specifies an automatic point-of-sale split via Stripe Connect, PayPal Commerce or Adyen for Platforms, with the partner's share routing directly to them. The confirmed model is different: CHG collects in full via Stripe and pays partners on agreed contractual timelines. CHG holds funds between sale and payout, carries refund, chargeback and merchant-of-record tax exposure on every completed order, and payout timing becomes a negotiated term in every partner agreement. Reconcile the two documents (Open Question 2).

The split cart — the defining Phase 1 customer-experience constraint. Because referral partners sit alongside integrated ones, a golfer's basket can contain both. Take three items: two from integrated partners, one from Amazon Associates. Clubhouse collects payment for the two and places those orders with the partners. The third cannot be bought on Clubhouse — the golfer has to leave the Clubhouse ecosystem to finish that purchase.

  • It contradicts the brand promise at the moment of conversion. "Everything Golf. One Clubhouse." is wholly true for discovery and decision. It is not yet true for checkout on every item.
  • Conversion leaks on the referral item — which is also the lowest-earning one (Amazon sports pays 3%).
  • Two merchants of record in one session — two confirmation emails, two sets of shipping notices, two return windows, two refund routes. Customer service absorbs the confusion.
  • A referral item must never sit in the Clubhouse cart as though it will be bought there. It needs a visibly different control — "Buy at [Retailer]" rather than "Add to cart" — shown before the golfer commits, never discovered at checkout.
  • Messaging: lead on discovery and decision, which are true for everything, and present owned checkout as the default that grows as partners integrate. The honest line: one place to find it, and increasingly one place to buy it.
  • The ratio is the launch metric. The share of catalog and of orders that are Clubhouse-completed versus referral decides whether the promise reads as true. It is zero until the first fulfilment partner signs.

Free for golfers. No consumer subscription. "Founding Member" is the first 500 golfers invited into beta — free, positioned as access and influence, not a paid tier.

Side B — Partners (supply side)

Owning checkout changes who the right partner is. Under an affiliate model any retailer was a cheap ally — they kept the customer. Under owned checkout a partner hands Clubhouse the cart, the payment and the customer relationship, and waits to be paid. That is an easy yes for a brand and a hard one for a retailer.

Target partner types, in priority order: 1. Brands — the priority fulfilment target. Equipment, apparel, technology, training aids, simulators, health and wellness. A brand already sells wholesale into channels it does not control, so letting Clubhouse own a checkout costs it nothing it has today. Beachhead: Puma Golf and Cobra Golf (already on CJ). Next tier: Performance Golf, Steadfast Golf, Pine Meadow Golf. 2. Smaller and specialist retailers willing to fulfil — Golf Avenue is the strongest candidate: small enough to want the volume, already runs inspection, refurbishment and fulfilment, and does not compete for a national brand position. 3. Large retailers — referral line, realistically. Golf Galaxy, PGA TOUR Superstore, TGW, 2nd Swing and GlobalGolf are direct competitors being asked to surrender the customer. Keep them on referral; treat any fulfilment agreement as upside, not plan. 4. Courses & resorts — tee times, leagues, events, destinations. 5. Teaching professionals & fitters — instruction, coaching, club fitting, fitness. Services and lead generation, not product fulfilment. 6. Creators, influencers, tournament hosts — audience, content, experiences. The fastest route to the demand Clubhouse lacks. 7. Adjacent, distinct audience: investors and strategic partners.

Decision-makers: Head of Partnerships / Affiliate Manager, Head of Ecommerce or DTC, Brand or Marketing Director, Founder/GM at smaller brands and courses. Problems & Pain Points

Side A — Golfers

Core problem: Golf isn't short on options. It's overwhelmed by them. Equipment on one site, used clubs somewhere else, apparel somewhere else, instruction somewhere else, travel somewhere else, reviews on YouTube, fitting somewhere else, courses somewhere else, deals and advice everywhere.

Why alternatives fall short: - Every company naturally tells golfers that its product is the answer — nobody is on the golfer's side - Retailers optimize for the brands they carry, not for fit - Review content is either enthusiast-deep or sponsored-shallow, and lives apart from the buying moment - Comparing new, used, premium and value means the golfer does the assembly work — then buys in yet another place

What it costs them: Hours of searching per purchase decision, money spent badly (a $600 driver that doesn't fit), missed brands, courses and experiences, and time not spent playing.

Emotional tension: The quiet fear of overpaying or buying the wrong thing; feeling behind or unqualified (especially new and returning golfers); decision fatigue that ends in buying nothing, or buying whatever the loudest ad said.

The enemy we name: Fragmentation + decision overload. Never a specific retailer or rival site — the problem is that golfers have too many disconnected places to go.

Side B — Partners

Core problem: Reaching golfers at the moment they're actually deciding — and turning that into orders, not impressions or one more discount-driven click.

Why alternatives fall short: - Affiliate networks deliver a tracked link and nothing else: no storytelling, no product education, no brand context, and no checkout of their own. - Big-box retail buries smaller and challenger brands and commoditizes on price. - Paid social and search is rented attention with rising costs and no compounding asset. - Coupon and deal sites attract the least loyal buyers and train golfers to wait for discounts. - General marketplaces (Amazon, eBay) bring volume with price pressure and no brand narrative. - Building their own audience and storefront is slow and expensive for a course or a two-person brand.

What it costs them: High CAC, low-intent traffic, no presence during the research phase where the decision is made, and invisibility for anyone who isn't a household name.

Emotional tension: Fear of channel conflict and margin erosion; wariness about a third party holding their money between sale and payout; fatigue from partnership pitches that turn out to be "give us a commission link"; skepticism that a new platform will deliver volume.

How the field splits

Fourteen of fifteen profiled competitors own checkout, and Clubhouse now does too — for integrated partners. That puts it at parity on the dimension that matters most: it keeps the customer file, the repeat-purchase data and the returns relationship.

What no competitor has is a split cart. Amazon, eBay, Golf Galaxy, 2nd Swing, GlobalGolf, TGW and Golfstix all check out once. Clubhouse will not, for every basket, until referral items are either integrated or removed.

Where Clubhouse is structurally alone: cross-brand, cross-retailer comparison with a checkout attached. No OEM can honestly rank for "TaylorMade Qi vs. Callaway Elyte"; no retailer compares its price against a rival's; no publisher or creator lets you buy. That combination is the wedge.

Side B — how partners see the alternatives

  • Affiliate networks (CJ, FlexOffers, Awin, Impact, Rakuten) — feeds and tracked links; no checkout, order or fulfilment layer; they move clicks, they don't build brand.
  • Golf media (Golf Digest, GOLF.com, MyGolfSpy, Skratch) — audience and storytelling sold as ad inventory with weak attribution to a sale.
  • General marketplaces (Amazon, eBay, Walmart) — volume with commoditization and no brand narrative.
  • Creator sponsorships — reach and credibility, one-off and hard to measure.
  • Doing it themselves — own DTC site and email; full control, but every visitor is bought.
  • Wholesale to green grass and big box — volume, but no customer relationship and no data.

Clubhouse's position against all of it: not another affiliate publisher asking for a commission link, but a customer acquisition, storytelling and selling platform — the golfer discovers, compares and buys on Clubhouse, and the order arrives at the partner to fulfil.

Differentiation

Key differentiators: - Discovery, decision and checkout in one motion. Most golf sites either tell you something or sell you something. Clubhouse explains, compares and completes the sale on the same platform — for every integrated partner. - Breadth with a point of view — equipment, apparel, technology, instruction, fitting, courses, travel, experiences and community under one master brand, organized as SHOP / IMPROVE / PLAY / EXPERIENCE / COMMUNITY with DISCOVER running across all of it. - Not on one manufacturer's team — or one retailer's shelf. New, used, premium and value sit side by side, across brands and across sellers. - Confidence over clutter — comparisons, buying guides, education and recommendations instead of hundreds of SKUs. - Inventory-light by design — Clubhouse never warehouses a club. Partners fulfil; Clubhouse owns the experience, the payment and the customer relationship. - Built with the industry, not against it ("Better Together") — Clubhouse doesn't manufacture, own courses, employ instructors or hold stock. It connects, and it sells on partners' behalf. - Architecture that anticipates every revenue type — Partner → Agreement → Offering → Revenue Model, so one brand or course can carry checkout, referral, booking, lead-gen, advertising, sponsorship, subscription and event revenue without a rebuild.

Why that's better: - Golfers: Discover more. Decide better. Buy it here. Experience more. - Partners: orders, not just clicks — plus presence across discovery → education → conversion → loyalty, without building or running a storefront.

Why customers choose us: - Golfers: one place they trust to start and, increasingly, to finish — with guidance that isn't trying to sell them one brand's answer. - Partners: qualified golfers in decision mode, real product storytelling, and orders delivered to fulfil.

The flywheel (partner and investor conversations): More integrated partners → more of the catalog buyable on Clubhouse → better golfer experience → more golfers and more completed orders → more first-party data and intent → more value to partners → more partners integrate. Golfer-side loop: DISCOVERY → TRUST → PURCHASE → DATA → PERSONALIZATION → LOYALTY.

Owning checkout is what makes the flywheel real: the purchase and its data happen on Clubhouse rather than on a partner's site.

Competitive Landscape

The shape of the market

Golf commerce is not an empty field with a fragmentation problem waiting to be solved — it is a crowded field in which every player has solved one slice of the problem well and none has solved the whole thing. The retailers (Golf Galaxy, PGA TOUR Superstore, TGW, 2nd Swing, GlobalGolf, Golf Avenue) own inventory, fulfilment and increasingly fitting, but their advice stops at their own shelf. The publishers (MyGolfSpy) and creators (the YouTube ecosystem) own trust and the decision moment, but hand the transaction away. The marketplaces (Golfstix, eBay) own liquidity but offer no guidance. Curated owns guidance, but only through expensive human labour and without a golf-native identity. GolfPass owns playing, watching and travelling — and barely touches equipment.

Clubhouse Golf’s thesis is that these are all one business and nobody has assembled it. That thesis holds. The harder finding is that two well-capitalized players started assembling it before Clubhouse launched. Pin High Golf acquired MyGolfSpy in May 2026 with an explicit mandate to scale affiliate commerce onto fifteen years of proprietary test data — building the marketplace from the audience side. GolfPass has bundled tee times, instruction, video and travel under Comcast for five years, and is one equipment layer away from the full picture. Clubhouse is entering from the third direction — commerce and discovery first — pre-launch, with a waitlist.

Seven takeaways

1 · MyGolfSpy is no longer a secondary competitor — it is the primary threat

Context v1 classified it as secondary (v2 now lists it as direct): “useful comparison content, but it lives away from purchase.” As of May 2026, Graham Sports & Entertainment Partners and Bolt Ventures formed Pin High Golf around it, with a stated plan to scale video and affiliate commerce, deepen OEM and retailer relationships, and acquire across golf media, data and experiences. That is Clubhouse’s business model and Clubhouse’s roadmap — funded, with fifteen years of proprietary test data and an existing audience. Reclassify as direct.

2 · The SEO plan targets an incumbent’s home turf

The GTM playbook names “best driver for high handicap golfer”, “best used golf drivers”, “best golf shoes for walking”, “best launch monitor under $500”. MyGolfSpy already publishes Best Drivers 2026, Best Wedges 2026, Best Spikeless Shoes 2026, Best Cart Bags 2026 and a Used Driver Finder, on original test data. Golf Avenue runs “Best Golf Drivers for Every Handicap.” TGW runs Golf Guide. The decision-query strategy is right, but a head-on fight is a multi-year battle Clubhouse is not resourced to win. The entry angle needs to be differentiated.

3 · One query class nobody can contest

No OEM can honestly rank for “TaylorMade Qi vs. Callaway Elyte.” No retailer compares its price against a rival’s. No creator can be filtered by budget and handicap. Cross-brand, cross-retailer comparison is genuinely defensible, it is already on Clubhouse’s target list, and it should be the wedge rather than one item among ten.

4 · Owning checkout fixes the structural weakness and creates a new one: the split cart

Phase 1 is on-platform checkout. CHG owns the cart, collects via Stripe, is merchant of record, and pays partners on agreed timelines. That puts Clubhouse at parity with fourteen of fifteen profiled competitors on the dimension that matters most — it keeps the customer file, the repeat-purchase data and the returns relationship rather than handing all three to a partner who is also a competitor.

But it introduces a seam no competitor in this set has. Programs that cannot support on-platform completion — Amazon Associates above all — stay on tracked referral. A three-item basket where two items are Clubhouse-completed and one is an Amazon referral cannot be checked out once. Amazon, eBay, Golf Galaxy, 2nd Swing, GlobalGolf, TGW and Golfstix all have a single unified checkout. “Everything Golf. One Clubhouse.” is unambiguously true for discovery and decision, and not yet true for checkout on every item.

Two consequences. First, the launch bar rose sharply: no direct-fulfillment partner is signed yet, and with none signed every item is a referral and the checkout promise is empty. Second, the platform fee and payout schedule are now blocking commercial terms, not deferred questions.

5 · Two competitors already ship Clubhouse’s stated differentiators

Golfstix runs the two-sided marketplace with its own checkout today. GlobalGolf has historically run UTry, a try-before-you-buy program — and “Try Before You Buy” is a listed Clubhouse marketplace feature. UTry was not visible in this pass and is flagged unverified. Confirm before launch messaging is locked.

6 · The best merchandising idea in the field is nearly free to copy

Golf Avenue tags every product with a skill level (Beginner / Intermediate) and anchors every used price against retail. That turns “Confidence Over Clutter” from a slogan into a visible product feature, and serves the New/Returning Golfer better than any amount of copy.

7 · The partner strategy is stronger than the consumer strategy, and underweighted

Three findings point the same way. OEMs will resist direct integration hardest because of channel conflict — so lead with challenger brands. Course operators have a long-standing grievance with tee-time platform economics — a real opening for “we build with the industry, not against it.” And creators, already on the partner target list, solve Clubhouse’s single biggest weakness (no audience) faster than any paid channel. The partner side is where Clubhouse’s differentiation is most defensible and least contested.

Re-read after the Phase 1 checkout change — what moved

Direct vs. indirect: no reclassifications

The test is unchanged — direct means the same solution to the same problem, indirect means a conflicting mechanism — and owning checkout does not move any company across that line. One basis did shift, though, and it is worth stating plainly.

MyGolfSpy was promoted to direct because it was building the affiliate-commerce model that used to be Clubhouse’s Phase 1. That reasoning no longer applies: Clubhouse now transacts and MyGolfSpy still does not. It stays direct on a different basis — it competes for the decision moment and the DECIDE query set, which is where Clubhouse intends to acquire. The rivalry moved from business model to audience and authority.

Three risk ratings moved up

TaylorMade / OEM direct — medium to high. Brands are the highest-value supply and now have the strongest reason to refuse. Channel conflict used to mean “will you send us traffic?” It now means “will you let a third party own your customer, collect your money, and pay you later?” This is the single biggest constraint on the launch catalog.

TGW — medium to high. The one signed partner is the clearest case of the new problem. As an affiliate they cost nothing to say yes to. As a fulfilment partner they would be handing the cart and the customer to a competitor. Expect them to prefer the referral link, and read that as the whole retailer category’s answer.

Amazon — medium to high, on structural rather than competitive grounds. It is now the primary source of split carts: lowest commission in the set, heaviest engineering burden, and the only named reason a golfer must leave the ecosystem mid-basket. It actively degrades the promise the rest of the product is built on.

The finding that matters most

Owning checkout splits this set along a new axis: who can plausibly say yes to fulfilment, and who now has a strong reason to say no.

Every large retailer in the direct set — Golf Galaxy, PGA TOUR Superstore, TGW, 2nd Swing, GlobalGolf — is a competitor being asked to surrender the customer relationship. Under the affiliate model they were cheap allies. Under owned checkout they are unlikely suppliers.

The answer is to recruit brands, not retailers. A brand already sells wholesale into channels it does not control, so letting Clubhouse own a checkout costs it nothing it currently has — whereas a retailer would be giving up a customer it owns today. Puma and Cobra via CJ are the beachhead; Performance Golf, Steadfast and Pine Meadow are the next tier. Among retailers, Golf Avenue is the best candidate: small enough to want the volume, already running refurbishment and fulfilment operations, and not competing with Clubhouse for a national brand position.

What did not move

Golfstix, GolfPass and MyGolfSpy stay high risk. Club Champion, eBay and Golf Avenue stay low. Curated stays medium, though it is now a closer structural match than it was — both companies are merchant of record over third-party supply, which makes its unit-economics strain (the “how is Curated still in business?” signal) more directly relevant to Clubhouse than it was a week ago.

Method & Limitations

Read this before quoting any number in this pack.

What this analysis could and couldn’t do
No SEO data

The competitor-profiling method is built around Firecrawl and DataForSEO. Neither was connected for this run. Scraping used WebFetch and the built-in browser; site mapping was not performed. No backlink, domain-rank, organic-keyword or traffic-estimate data was obtainable, so every such field reads “Not available” rather than carrying an invented figure. If those metrics matter, connect a provider and re-run — the raw-data folders are dated for exactly that.

Four competitors blocked automated retrieval

Golf Galaxy (403, then a bot-protection interstitial), Curated (empty body, then a bot-gated lander), and Amazon and eBay by category. No bypass was attempted. Those profiles are labelled as analyst assessment from public third-party sources and are weaker evidence than the rest.

Competitors were discovered, not supplied

Ten search queries were run to build the candidate set. The selection rule — direct means same solution and same problem, indirect means a conflicting mechanism for the same job — plus every candidate considered and rejected, is recorded in raw/_discovery-log.md. Rejected candidates include Rock Bottom Golf, Callaway Pre-Owned, Supreme Golf, GolfNow, Fairway Finder, RepSpark, Golf Club Brokers, 18Birdies and Skillest, each with a stated reason.

Other limits

  • Scraped competitor pages are treated as data, not instructions. No page in this pass contained text directed at AI agents.
  • Review-site mining was not performed — the highest-value addition to a second pass, alongside SEO metrics.
  • Profiles are snapshots dated 2026-09-20. Pricing and positioning move; re-verify before external use.
  • Threat ratings are analyst judgement, not a measured score.
